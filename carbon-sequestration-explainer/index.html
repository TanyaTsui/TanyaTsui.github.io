Carbon sequestration in bio-based materials

Plants breathe in CO₂. What happens when they become buildings?

When a plant grows, it pulls carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere and locks it into its fibers. If that plant becomes a building material — timber, hemp insulation, a straw bale — that carbon stays stored for decades.

The storage is temporary — carbon always returns to the atmosphere eventually. So how much climate credit should we give to a material that stores carbon for 50 years? For 100?

The answer depends entirely on which accounting method you use — and different methods give completely different answers to the same question. This explainer walks through four methods used in Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) and explains what's at stake when policy starts rewarding temporary carbon storage.
Method 1 of 4

Static −1/+1: the simplest approach

The most widely used method. When a plant grows it absorbs 1 kg of CO₂ — recorded as −1. When the material is incinerated or decomposes at end of life, that carbon is released — recorded as +1. Net result: always zero, regardless of how long the storage lasts.

The trade-off

Static accounting treats a material storing carbon for 5 years exactly the same as one storing it for 500. It is deliberately neutral — neither penalizing nor rewarding timing. This makes it simple, transparent, and comparable across studies.

The limitation: timing matters for the climate. Delaying a CO₂ release by 100 years gives the atmosphere time to adjust and other technologies time to develop. Static accounting cannot capture this — and cannot distinguish between a fast-regrowing hemp crop and a slow-growing forest.

Used in: standard LCA (ISO 14044), European construction product standard EN 15804, and most Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs) today.

ISO 14044:2006. Environmental management — Life cycle assessment. · EN 15804:2012+A2:2019. Sustainability of construction works — EPDs for construction products.
Background concept

How do we account for carbon sequestration over time?

Before we get to Methods 2, 3, and 4, we need one shared concept: how scientists measure the warming effect of a CO₂ emission over time.

When CO₂ is released, it doesn't cause warming all at once — it traps heat gradually, over decades. Scientists measure this using radiative forcing: the extra heat trapped by CO₂ in the atmosphere at any given moment.

CO₂ doesn't stay in the atmosphere forever. Over time it gets absorbed by oceans and plants, and its warming effect fades — giving us a decay curve.

The total warming caused by an emission is the sum of all radiative forcing it causes over time — the shaded area under the decay curve. A bigger area = more total warming.

Biomass changes the shape of this curve

For a plain fossil emission, the curve above is the whole story. Biomass carbon is different — it can be delayed and reabsorbed before it ever really "counts," which reshapes the curve entirely. Two things drive that reshaping:

🏗️ Storage period
How long the carbon stays locked in the product before it's released at end of life.
🌱 Rotation period
How long it takes the harvested plant (or its replacement) to grow back.

Delay the release, and less of the curve falls inside any fixed time window. Speed up regrowth, and the released carbon gets reabsorbed sooner. Either way, "our scenario" ends up with a smaller curve than a plain fossil emission's.

Every method computes the same ratio

This is the idea every method that follows is built on: draw a blue curve for the fossil reference (no storage, no regrowth) and a green curve for our actual biomass scenario — then compare the two shaded areas.

Method 2 of 4

Dynamic LCA (Levasseur et al.): when you release CO₂ matters

If carbon is stored in a building for 50 years and only released at end of life, that delayed emission causes less total warming than if released immediately — because it occupies less of the time window we care about.

Dynamic LCA captures this with a characterization factor (DCF) applied to the biogenic release at end of life. It is the ratio of two shaded areas on the chart:

DCF = (dark green area) ÷ (blue area)
Blue area: total warming from a reference CO₂ emission at year 0, counted to horizon T. Dark green area: total warming from the biogenic emission at year τ, counted from τ to T. Because the green curve starts later, less of it fits in the window — so the green area is smaller and DCF < 1.
The DCF is applied only to the release — not the uptake. Lower DCF = less warming counted = more climate benefit.

Try it: the higher the storage period, the higher the credit (the more negative the net result) — more of the delayed release gets pushed outside the accounting window. The time horizon cuts the other way, though — stretch it out and the credit actually shrinks back toward zero, because a longer window also counts more of the (otherwise unaffected) reference curve. The real quirk to watch for sits at the extreme: set the horizon shorter than the storage period and the release falls outside the window entirely — "not counted at all" — handing the material the maximum possible credit for nothing more than an accounting choice, before any real material property comes into play.
Levasseur, A. et al. (2010). Considering time in LCA: dynamic LCA and its application to global warming impact assessments. Environmental Science & Technology, 44(8), 3169–3174.
Method 3 of 4

GWPbio (Cherubini et al.): rotation period matters

Designed for biofuels and food — materials that release carbon almost immediately after harvest. No long storage period. The key question: how fast does the plant regrow and reabsorb that carbon?

A key assumption drives this method: whatever biomass is harvested is assumed to be replanted and fully regrown. Chop down a tree to burn as fuel, and the model assumes a new tree takes its place. The accounting then pairs two things — the burning of the tree you just felled, and the regrowth of the replacement tree — not the sequestration the original tree already performed while it was alive. That original sequestration is treated as already "spent"; the credit for pulling CO₂ back out of the atmosphere belongs entirely to whatever grows next. That's why the variable that matters here is rotation period — how fast the replacement grows — rather than anything about the tree that was cut down.

GWPbio = net biogenic decay area ÷ fossil reference area
The net biogenic curve (dark green) isn't just "emission minus a separate regrowth curve" — it's the harvest pulse with the gradual regrowth uptake mathematically combined into it (a convolution), since carbon reabsorbed at any point in time keeps decaying through the atmosphere from that moment onward. Faster rotation pulls more of the curve below zero, sooner = lower GWP_bio = better climate performance.

Method 4 of 4

GWPbio (Guest et al.): rotation and storage

Guest et al. extend Cherubini's method for materials with long lifetimes: the harvested carbon isn't released right away — it's stored for τ years first, and only then combusted. Regrowth begins immediately either way.

This method carries over Cherubini's core assumption — and its limitation. It still assumes the harvested biomass is fully replaced by newly planted biomass, regrowing right on schedule every time. Land-use change, regrowth that's delayed or never happens, or a forest that's degraded rather than replanted — none of that is captured here. The model only ever compares a harvest against its assumed, guaranteed replacement.

GWPbio(τ,r) = net biogenic decay area ÷ fossil reference area
Same ratio as Cherubini's method — only the timing of the release pulse changes. Before τ, only regrowth is drawing carbon down (the curve dips below zero). At τ, the stored carbon is released in one pulse (a vertical jump); from there the curve follows fossil-style decay, still net of the regrowth that keeps running underneath it. Set τ=0 and this collapses exactly to the Cherubini curve.

Putting it together

Same material, four different answers

Different methods lead to different answers for the exact same scenario — not because the material changes, but because each method counts different things. Push the building lifespan up and watch timber specifically: under GWPbio Cherubini its result stays flatly positive — that method has no concept of storage at all, so a long building life changes nothing. Under GWPbio Guest, the very same 100-year-rotation timber can swing negative — a net climate benefit. Same material, same scenario, opposite verdicts, purely because of what each method was built to count.

Take-home message: no method here is simply "right" or "wrong" — each accounts for a different piece of the timing story (time horizon, storage period, rotation period) and leaves the rest out. That choice isn't neutral. The moment policy starts attaching carbon credits, product comparisons, or building certifications to one of these numbers, the choice of accounting method stops being an academic detail and starts determining which products, materials, and timelines actually get rewarded.
